1.1 Intuitive Definition of a Limit

Informally, the limit of a function $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches a value $a$ is the value that $f(x)$ gets arbitrarily close to as $x$ gets arbitrarily close to (but not necessarily equal to) $a$.

📐 Definition — Limit
We write $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$ and say "the limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $a$ equals $L$" if we can make the values of $f(x)$ as close to $L$ as we like by taking $x$ close enough to $a$ (but not equal to $a$).

Notice: the limit does not depend on $f(a)$. The function may be undefined at $x = a$, and the limit can still exist. What matters is the behavior near $a$.

Figure 1.1 The limit idea. As $x$ approaches $a$ from either side, $f(x)$ approaches $L$ — regardless of whether $f(a)$ exists (open circle).
🔑 Key Insight
The function value $f(a)$ is irrelevant to the limit. Even if $f(a) \ne L$ or $f(a)$ is undefined, $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ can equal $L$.

1.2 One-Sided Limits

Sometimes a function approaches different values depending on the direction of approach.

Figure 1.2 One-sided limits disagree. The two pieces approach different heights at $x=2$, so $\lim_{x\to2}f(x)$ does not exist — even though $f(2)$ is defined.
📐 Definition — One-Sided Limits

The left-hand limit: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L$ means $f(x) \to L$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from the left ($x < a$).

The right-hand limit: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L$ means $f(x) \to L$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from the right ($x > a$).

✅ Theorem — Two-Sided Limit Exists iff Both One-Sided Limits Agree
$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \quad \iff \quad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L \;\text{ and }\; \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L$$

Example: For $f(x) = \dfrac{|x|}{x}$:

$$\lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{|x|}{x} = \frac{-x}{x} = -1, \qquad \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{|x|}{x} = \frac{x}{x} = 1$$

Since $-1 \ne 1$, the two-sided limit $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{|x|}{x}$ does not exist.

1.3 Limit Laws & Algebraic Techniques

✅ Limit Laws
If $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x\to a} g(x) = M$, then:
LawStatement
Sum$\lim_{x\to a}[f(x)+g(x)] = L + M$
Difference$\lim_{x\to a}[f(x)-g(x)] = L - M$
Constant Multiple$\lim_{x\to a}[c\cdot f(x)] = cL$
Product$\lim_{x\to a}[f(x)\cdot g(x)] = L \cdot M$
Quotient$\lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \dfrac{L}{M}$, provided $M \ne 0$
Power$\lim_{x\to a}[f(x)]^n = L^n$
Root$\lim_{x\to a}\sqrt[n]{f(x)} = \sqrt[n]{L}$ (when defined)

Direct Substitution

For polynomial and rational functions (where the denominator is non-zero at $a$), you can simply substitute:

$$\lim_{x \to 3}(x^2 + 2x - 1) = 9 + 6 - 1 = 14$$

Indeterminate Form $\frac{0}{0}$

When direct substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$, try algebraic manipulation:

🛠 Techniques for $\frac{0}{0}$
  • Factor and cancel — factor numerator and denominator, cancel the common factor.
  • Multiply by conjugate — useful when radicals are involved.
  • Trig identities — use $\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \to 1$ and $\dfrac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta} \to 0$ as $\theta \to 0$.

Key trig limits (memorize):

$$\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1 \qquad \lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta} = 0 \qquad \lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\tan\theta}{\theta} = 1$$

1.4 Limits at Infinity & Horizontal Asymptotes

📐 Definition
$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$ means $f(x)$ approaches $L$ as $x$ grows without bound. When this equals $L$, the line $y = L$ is a horizontal asymptote.

Rational Functions at Infinity

Divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator:

Figure 1.3 Horizontal asymptote. As $x\to\pm\infty$ the graph hugs the dashed line $y=2$ — so $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{2x^2}{x^2+1}=2$.
$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 - 5x}{2x^2 + 1} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3 - \frac{5}{x}}{2 + \frac{1}{x^2}} = \frac{3-0}{2+0} = \frac{3}{2}$$
✅ Degree Rules for Rational Functions
For $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ where $\deg p = m$, $\deg q = n$:
  • If $m < n$: limit $= 0$ (horizontal asymptote $y = 0$)
  • If $m = n$: limit $= \dfrac{\text{leading coeff of }p}{\text{leading coeff of }q}$
  • If $m > n$: limit $= \pm\infty$ (no horizontal asymptote)

Vertical Asymptotes

A vertical asymptote at $x = a$ occurs when $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \pm\infty$ or $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \pm\infty$.

Example: $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x-3}$ has a vertical asymptote at $x=3$ because as $x \to 3^+$, $f(x) \to +\infty$.

1.5 The Squeeze Theorem

✅ Squeeze Theorem (Sandwich Theorem)
If $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ for all $x$ near $a$ (except possibly at $a$), and $$\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = \lim_{x \to a} h(x) = L,$$ then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$.

Classic application:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \sin\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = 0$$

Since $-1 \le \sin(1/x) \le 1$, we have $-x^2 \le x^2\sin(1/x) \le x^2$. Both $\pm x^2 \to 0$ as $x\to 0$, so by the Squeeze Theorem, the limit is $0$.

Figure 1.4 Squeeze Theorem. $f$ is trapped between $g$ and $h$; since both bounds head to the same limit, so must $f$.

1.6 Continuity

📐 Definition — Continuity at a Point
$f$ is continuous at $x = a$ if ALL THREE conditions hold:
  1. $f(a)$ is defined
  2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists
  3. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$
Failure of any condition produces a discontinuity.

Types of Discontinuities

Removable

The limit exists but $f(a) \ne \lim f$ or $f(a)$ undefined. A "hole" in the graph.

$f(x) = \dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ at $x=1$

Jump

Left and right limits both exist but are not equal.

$f(x) = \lfloor x \rfloor$ (floor function) at any integer

Infinite

The function approaches $\pm\infty$ at a point (vertical asymptote).

$f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ at $x=0$

Continuity on an Interval

$f$ is continuous on an open interval $(a,b)$ if it is continuous at every point in $(a,b)$.

Figure 1.5 Classifying discontinuities. Open circles mark values the graph approaches but does not reach; filled circles mark the actual value of $f$.

$f$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a,b]$ if it is continuous on $(a,b)$, the right-hand limit at $a$ equals $f(a)$, and the left-hand limit at $b$ equals $f(b)$.

📌 Continuous Function Families
These function types are continuous everywhere on their domains: Polynomials, rational functions (where denominator $\ne 0$), radical functions (where defined), trigonometric functions (where defined), exponential functions $a^x$ ($a>0$), and logarithmic functions (where $x > 0$).

1.7 Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

✅ Intermediate Value Theorem
If $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$ and $N$ is any number strictly between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there exists at least one number $c$ in $(a, b)$ such that $f(c) = N$.

Intuitively: a continuous function cannot "jump over" a value — it must pass through every intermediate value.

Figure 1.6 Intermediate Value Theorem. A continuous graph cannot skip a height: the line $y=N$ between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ must be crossed at some $c\in(a,b)$.
📝 Application — Showing a Root Exists
Show that $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$ has a root in $(1, 2)$.

$f(1) = 1 - 1 - 1 = -1 < 0$ and $f(2) = 8 - 2 - 1 = 5 > 0$. Since $f$ is a polynomial (continuous everywhere), and $0$ is between $f(1) = -1$ and $f(2) = 5$, the IVT guarantees a $c \in (1,2)$ with $f(c) = 0$.

Worked Examples

Example 1 Factoring to Evaluate a Limit

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}$.

1
Try direct substitution: Plug $x=3$: $\dfrac{9-9}{3-3} = \dfrac{0}{0}$ — indeterminate form.
2
Factor: $$\frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3} = \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3}$$
3
Cancel the common factor (valid since $x \ne 3$ in a limit): $$= x + 3$$
4
Substitute: $$\lim_{x \to 3}(x+3) = 3+3 = \boxed{6}$$
Example 2 Conjugate Technique with Radicals

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 4} \frac{\sqrt{x} - 2}{x - 4}$.

1
Direct substitution gives $\dfrac{0}{0}$ — indeterminate.
2
Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate $(\sqrt{x}+2)$: $$\frac{\sqrt{x}-2}{x-4} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x}+2}{\sqrt{x}+2} = \frac{x - 4}{(x-4)(\sqrt{x}+2)}$$
3
Cancel $(x-4)$: $$= \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+2}$$
4
Substitute $x = 4$: $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{4}+2} = \frac{1}{4} = \boxed{\tfrac{1}{4}}$$
Example 3 Trig Limit

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(3x)}{5x}$.

1
Use the known result $\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1$. Rewrite: $$\frac{\sin(3x)}{5x} = \frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{\sin(3x)}{3x}$$
2
As $x \to 0$, also $3x \to 0$, so $\dfrac{\sin(3x)}{3x} \to 1$.
3
$$\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin(3x)}{5x} = \frac{3}{5} \cdot 1 = \boxed{\dfrac{3}{5}}$$
Example 4 Limit at Infinity

Find $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{4x^3 - 2x}{6x^3 + x^2 + 5}$.

1
Divide every term by $x^3$ (the highest degree in denominator): $$\frac{4 - \tfrac{2}{x^2}}{6 + \tfrac{1}{x} + \tfrac{5}{x^3}}$$
2
As $x \to \infty$, all terms with $x$ in the denominator → 0: $$\frac{4 - 0}{6 + 0 + 0} = \boxed{\dfrac{2}{3}}$$
Example 5 Identifying Discontinuities

Classify all discontinuities of $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - x - 6}{x^2 - 4}$.

1
Factor: $\dfrac{(x-3)(x+2)}{(x-2)(x+2)}$. Denominator is zero at $x = 2$ and $x = -2$.
2
At $x = -2$: factor $(x+2)$ appears in both — cancel it. The limit exists: $$\lim_{x\to -2}\frac{x-3}{x-2} = \frac{-5}{-4} = \frac{5}{4}$$ But $f(-2)$ is undefined → removable discontinuity at $x = -2$.
3
At $x = 2$: after canceling, $f(x) \to \dfrac{x-3}{x-2}$, and as $x\to 2$, numerator $\to -1$, denominator $\to 0$ → $f(x) \to \pm\infty$. Infinite (vertical asymptote) discontinuity at $x = 2$.
Example 6 Piecewise Function — Does the Limit Exist?

Let $f(x) = \begin{cases} 2x + 1 & x < 1 \\ 4 & x = 1 \\ x^2 + 2 & x > 1 \end{cases}$. Does $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1} f(x)$ exist? Is $f$ continuous at $x=1$?

1
Left-hand limit: $\lim_{x\to 1^-}(2x+1) = 2(1)+1 = 3$
2
Right-hand limit: $\lim_{x\to 1^+}(x^2+2) = 1+2 = 3$
3
Both one-sided limits equal 3, so $\lim_{x\to 1}f(x) = 3$ exists.
4
But $f(1) = 4 \ne 3$. So the third condition of continuity fails. The function has a removable discontinuity at $x=1$.
